# Postscript

In 2021, when the X-Press Pearl disaster happened off the coast of Sri Lanka, I was there and observed how the impacts happened in real life. But in the beginning, most of the focus was on chemicals, and how the nurdles carried the chemicals. There was little attention to the nurdle spill by itself. Then, in 2024, I was at the UN headquarters in New York City on a reporting fellowship, and I spoke with Inger Andersen, the executive director of the UN Environment Programme (UNEP), about nurdle spills. It was right before the expected signing of the global plastics treaty (it's still not signed), but she told me about how the treaty is aiming to reduce plastic production at the source, and how this may reduce similar maritime accidents.

This made me more curious about nurdles, so after returning to Colombo, I checked whether nurdles were still there, on the beaches. They were hard to detect in the beginning, but after digging for a few minutes, I found plenty buried in the sand.

In 2025, I was again reporting on nurdle trade and spills in Asia, and two more ship accidents occurred off southern India. I saw fresh nurdles washing ashore with the monsoon winds and tides while I stood on the beach. Then, I traveled along the western and northwestern coast of Sri Lanka, chasing the new nurdles arriving on the beaches and documenting their locations.

When I was editing my draft and wanted to verify the volume of the nurdles on board one of the ships from the 2025 accidents, I realized that the reported numbers were not accurate. Then I got hold of the cargo manifest of that ship to add up the actual volumes being transported; this is when I realized just how many different types of nurdles there are.

When I arrived at Columbia last year, I had this grand idea in my head; I thought I would be able to get hold of lots of cargo manifests and then analyze them to understand the volumes of nurdles being transported. But after looking around for a while, I realized that cargo manifests are not so easy to get hold of.

I still wanted to work on this story about nurdle spills, and after doing some desk research and speaking to advocacy groups, I realized that these spills take place at different points in the value chain. Nurdles are spilled at the manufacturing facilities, during packaging, during conversion into secondary products, and during transportation.

I did some more desk research, found court cases and reached out to the defendants. I also reached out to the companies and authorities, then read a number of research papers to understand the ecological and health impacts.

This was a story where data was scarce, so I had to look around for data. I created an animated globe showing all the places where nurdles were discovered with the crowdsourced dataset from Nurdle Patrol quoted in the story. I also used the Our World in Data datasets and projections to create a chart on plastic production volumes over the years. I built a small dataset to create a map of the locations of maritime nurdle spills from 2020 to 2026. If I had more time, I would have liked to analyze and visualize nurdle trade data from the UN Comtrade database.

Overall, I am grateful for the many reporting trips and fellowships that fed into this. The data journalism skills I learned at Columbia brought it all together to make this a very interesting project!
