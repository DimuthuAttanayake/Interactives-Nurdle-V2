How a five-millimetre plastic pellet travels from the refinery to the far corners of the ocean, and never really leaves.

Fig. 01Meet the nurdle
Meet the nurdle

Plastics as we know them, from water bottles to cars, are created from small, translucent plastic polymers called “nurdles.”

Direction
  • Open the story on a single nurdle.
  • Round (not elliptical), translucent, off-white pellet.
Fig. 02How small?
How small?

These are approximately 5 mm in size, about the size of a lentil.

Direction
  • Show the dimension cleanly, a measured diameter, not a caliper.
  • Animate only the lines drawing in; no unnecessary rotation.
  • “5 mm” fades in with the line.
Fig. 03Where they are born
Where they are born

Plastics are a byproduct of oil and gas production, leftover chemicals from refining the oil and gas are used to manufacture nurdles.

Direction
  • Refinery visual; zoom into the polymer plant.
  • Flare / chimney kept still.
  • Nurdles move inside the plant, as if produced by invisible machinery.

Fig. 05.1Rail derailment
05.0 · Along the supply chain

Nurdles are released to the environment as a result of routine handling failures and accidents along the supply chain. They are a lesser-known form of industrial pollution, and one of the most pervasive forms of microplastic pollution globally.

Direction
  • Opener for the accidents / pollution theme.
05.1 · Rail derailment

For example, this happened during rail accidents, spilling the nurdles to the environment and often ending up in the water systems.

Direction
  • Clearer derailment: a hopper tips off the track.
  • Pellets spill out of the rail car one by one.
Fig. 05.2Shipping accident
05.2 · Shipping accident

Nurdle spills are now called the hallmark of modern ship disasters.

07.2 · Breaks into microplastics

…further disintegrating into smaller plastics…

07.3 · Ingested

…and surviving beyond a human lifespan.

Direction
  • They persist far beyond a human lifespan and are ingested by birds and fish.
The nurdle’s journey

One pellet, and almost none of it ever goes away.

Every nurdle that escapes outlasts the people who made it, breaking into ever-smaller plastic that lingers in the water for generations.
